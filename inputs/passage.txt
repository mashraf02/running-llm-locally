Renewable energy has moved from the margins of the global power system to its centre 
in less than two decades. Solar photovoltaic costs have fallen by roughly ninety 
percent since 2010, and onshore wind is now the cheapest source of new electricity in 
many countries. As a result, most new generating capacity added worldwide each year 
is renewable. Yet the transition faces real obstacles. Solar and wind output varies 
with the weather, so grids need storage, flexible demand and stronger transmission 
links to keep supply stable. Battery prices have dropped sharply, but storing energy 
for days or seasons remains expensive. Many developing countries also struggle to 
finance large projects because borrowing costs are high and investors perceive 
political and currency risks. Supply chains are concentrated, with a few countries 
dominating the manufacture of panels, batteries and critical minerals such as lithium 
and cobalt, which raises concerns about resilience and fair access. Governments are 
responding with a mix of tools: auctions that lock in low prices, carbon pricing, 
grid modernisation programmes and international climate finance. Analysts broadly 
agree that the technology is ready; the harder questions now concern money, 
infrastructure and coordination between countries. How quickly these gaps close will 
largely determine whether global emissions targets remain within reach.
